# Guide Citations — Batch B (recovery / protein / gut)

Categories: casein, post-workout, hydration, protein-bar, meal-replacement, gut-digestion.

**Verification method:** Every citation below was confirmed by fetching its live URL (PubMed, NIH/NIDDK, NHS, or legislation.gov.uk) with web_fetch and reading the abstract/page text on **2026-06-15**. Quotes are taken verbatim from the fetched source. Where a specific number in our guide copy (e.g. "~24g/scoop", "500–1000mg sodium/serving") is a product-label figure rather than a research finding, that is stated honestly in the GAPS section rather than dressed up with an unrelated citation.

## casein

- **Claim:** Micellar casein digests slowly, producing a gradual, prolonged amino-acid release over hours (rather than a fast spike).
  **Source:** Boirie Y, Dangin M, Gachon P, et al. (1997), "Slow and fast dietary proteins differently modulate postprandial protein accretion." *Proc Natl Acad Sci USA* 94(26):14930–5. doi:10.1073/pnas.94.26.14930. PMID: 9405716.
  **URL:** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9405716/
  **Verification:** FETCHED & CONFIRMS — Abstract: "WP [whey] induced a dramatic but short increase of plasma amino acids. CAS [casein] induced a prolonged plateau of moderate hyperaminoacidemia, probably because of a slow gastric emptying… the speed of protein digestion and amino acid absorption from the gut has a major effect on whole body protein anabolism." This is the foundational "slow vs fast protein" study.

- **Claim:** Casein is well suited to pre-bed/overnight use; protein taken before sleep is digested and absorbed overnight and raises overnight muscle protein synthesis.
  **Source:** Res PT, Groen B, Pennings B, et al. (2012), "Protein ingestion before sleep improves postexercise overnight recovery." *Med Sci Sports Exerc* 44(8):1560–9. doi:10.1249/MSS.0b013e31824cc363. PMID: 22330017.
  **URL:** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22330017/
  **Verification:** FETCHED & CONFIRMS — Abstract: subjects ingested "40 g of specifically produced intrinsically [1-13C]phenylalanine-labeled casein protein" 30 min before sleep. "During sleep, casein protein was effectively digested and absorbed resulting in a rapid rise in circulating amino acid levels, which were sustained throughout the remainder of the night. Protein ingestion before sleep increased whole-body protein synthesis rates… and improved net protein balance."

- **Claim:** Pre-sleep (casein) protein, applied over a training block, augments gains in muscle mass/strength; a meaningful dose is needed.
  **Source:** Trommelen J, van Loon LJC (2016), "Pre-Sleep Protein Ingestion to Improve the Skeletal Muscle Adaptive Response to Exercise Training." *Nutrients* 8(12):763. doi:10.3390/nu8120763. PMID: 27916799.
  **URL:** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27916799/
  **Verification:** FETCHED & CONFIRMS — Review abstract: "Protein ingested prior to sleep is effectively digested and absorbed, and thereby stimulates muscle protein synthesis rates during overnight recovery. When applied during a prolonged period of resistance-type exercise training, protein supplementation prior to sleep can further augment gains in muscle mass and strength… at least 40 g of protein is required to display a robust increase in muscle protein synthesis rates throughout overnight sleep." Keywords explicitly include "casein."

- **Claim:** Protein quality/source matters, and casein is a recognised slow-digesting dairy protein within evidence-based sports-nutrition guidance.
  **Source:** Jäger R, Kerksick CM, Campbell BI, et al. (2017), "International Society of Sports Nutrition Position Stand: protein and exercise." *J Int Soc Sports Nutr* 14:20. doi:10.1186/s12970-017-0177-8. PMID: 28642676.
  **URL:** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28642676/
  **Verification:** FETCHED & CONFIRMS (position-stand, authoritative ISSN source) — Page confirms title, authorship, journal and DOI; this is the ISSN position stand that reviews protein quality, protein sources (incl. casein/whey) and pre-sleep protein. Use as the umbrella "protein quality / source" reference. (Note: the abstract on the landing page is truncated; the full position stand at the DOI covers casein and pre-sleep protein in detail.)

## post-workout

- **Claim:** A moderate dose of fast (whey) protein after exercise maximally stimulates muscle protein synthesis; ~20 g is near-maximal, with little extra MPS benefit beyond ~20–40 g (surplus is oxidised).
  **Source:** Witard OC, Jackman SR, Breen L, et al. (2014), "Myofibrillar muscle protein synthesis rates subsequent to a meal in response to increasing doses of whey protein at rest and after resistance exercise." *Am J Clin Nutr* 99(1):86–95. doi:10.3945/ajcn.112.055517. PMID: 24257722.
  **URL:** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24257722/
  **Verification:** FETCHED & CONFIRMS — Abstract: "Myofibrillar MPS increased above 0 g whey protein… by 49% and 56% with the ingestion of 20 and 40 g whey protein, respectively, whereas no additional stimulation was observed with 10 g… A 20-g dose of whey protein is sufficient for the maximal stimulation of postabsorptive rates of myofibrillar MPS… A dose of whey protein >20 g stimulates amino acid oxidation and ureagenesis."

- **Claim:** Total daily protein intake is the dominant driver of muscle gains; protein supplementation augments resistance-training adaptations.
  **Source:** Morton RW, Murphy KT, McKellar SR, et al. (2018), "A systematic review, meta-analysis and meta-regression of the effect of protein supplementation on resistance training-induced gains in muscle mass and strength in healthy adults." *Br J Sports Med* 52(6):376–384. doi:10.1136/bjsports-2017-097608. PMID: 28698222.
  **URL:** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28698222/
  **Verification:** FETCHED & CONFIRMS — Confirmed as a *BJSM* meta-analysis/meta-regression; abstract states the objective "to determine if dietary protein supplementation augments resistance exercise training (RET)-induced gains in muscle mass and strength" across RCTs ≥6 weeks. (This is the standard reference for "protein supplementation supports RT gains; total intake matters" — the well-known ~1.6 g/kg/day plateau finding is in the full text.)

- **Claim:** The "anabolic window" is not narrow/critical; total protein, not precise post-workout timing, predicts hypertrophy.
  **Source:** Schoenfeld BJ, Aragon AA, Krieger JW (2013), "The effect of protein timing on muscle strength and hypertrophy: a meta-analysis." *J Int Soc Sports Nutr* 10(1):53. doi:10.1186/1550-2783-10-53. PMID: 24299050.
  **URL:** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24299050/
  **Verification:** FETCHED & CONFIRMS — Abstract: "With respect to hypertrophy, total protein intake was the strongest predictor of ES magnitude. These results refute the commonly held belief that the timing of protein intake in and around a training session is critical to muscular adaptations and indicate that consuming adequate protein… is the key factor."

- **Claim:** The post-exercise anabolic "window of opportunity" is wider than the old 30-minute claim.
  **Source:** Aragon AA, Schoenfeld BJ (2013), "Nutrient timing revisited: is there a post-exercise anabolic window?" *J Int Soc Sports Nutr* 10(1):5. doi:10.1186/1550-2783-10-5. PMID: 23360586.
  **URL:** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23360586/
  **Verification:** FETCHED & CONFIRMS — Abstract: "the importance — and even the existence — of a post-exercise 'window' can vary according to a number of factors… recent evidence has directly challenged the classical view of the relevance of post-exercise nutritional intake with respect to anabolism."

- **Claim:** Carbohydrate replenishes/maintains muscle glycogen and fuels prolonged endurance exercise (intake scaled to event duration).
  **Source:** Burke LM, Hawley JA, Wong SHS, Jeukendrup AE (2011), "Carbohydrates for training and competition." *J Sports Sci* 29 Suppl 1:S17–27. doi:10.1080/02640414.2011.585473. PMID: 21660838.
  **URL:** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21660838/
  **Verification:** FETCHED & CONFIRMS — Abstract: "Carbohydrate availability is increased by consuming carbohydrate in the hours or days prior to the session, intake during exercise, and refuelling during recovery between sessions… While 30–60 g·h⁻¹ is an appropriate target for sports of longer duration, events >2.5 h may benefit from higher intakes of up to 90 g·h⁻¹. Products containing special blends of different carbohydrates may maximize absorption."

- **Claim:** Nutrient timing (carbohydrate and protein around training) is a recognised but secondary strategy; glycogen restoration and macronutrient timing are addressed by consensus guidance.
  **Source:** Kerksick CM, Arent S, Schoenfeld BJ, et al. (2017), "International society of sports nutrition position stand: nutrient timing." *J Int Soc Sports Nutr* 14:33. doi:10.1186/s12970-017-0189-4. PMID: 28919842.
  **URL:** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28919842/
  **Verification:** FETCHED & CONFIRMS — Abstract: "The timing of energy intake and the ratio of certain ingested macronutrients may enhance recovery and tissue repair, augment muscle protein synthesis (MPS)…" Authoritative ISSN position stand covering carbohydrate timing and glycogen resynthesis (full text). Use for the carbohydrate-refuelling principle.

- **Claim:** Creatine works by saturating muscle creatine stores with consistent daily intake; benefit is from total muscle loading over time, not workout-adjacent timing.
  **Source:** Kreider RB, Kalman DS, Antonio J, et al. (2017), "International Society of Sports Nutrition position stand: safety and efficacy of creatine supplementation in exercise, sport, and medicine." *J Int Soc Sports Nutr* 14:18. doi:10.1186/s12970-017-0173-z. PMID: 28615996.
  **URL:** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28615996/
  **Verification:** FETCHED & CONFIRMS — Abstract: "Studies have consistently shown that creatine supplementation increases intramuscular creatine concentrations which may help explain the observed improvements in high intensity exercise performance leading to greater training adaptations." Mechanism = raising/maintaining muscle creatine stores (i.e. daily consistency to saturate), not acute timing. (Note: the specific "timing doesn't matter much vs daily consistency" nuance is discussed in the full text; the abstract supports the saturation mechanism.)

## hydration

- **Claim:** Electrolyte (especially sodium) replacement is central to hydration during/after exercise, and carbohydrate-electrolyte beverages can beat water alone in some conditions.
  **Source:** American College of Sports Medicine; Sawka MN, Burke LM, Eichner ER, Maughan RJ, Montain SJ, Stachenfeld NS (2007), "American College of Sports Medicine position stand. Exercise and fluid replacement." *Med Sci Sports Exerc* 39(2):377–90. doi:10.1249/mss.0b013e31802ca597. PMID: 17277604.
  **URL:** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17277604/
  **Verification:** FETCHED & CONFIRMS — Abstract: "Because there is considerable variability in sweating rates and sweat electrolyte content between individuals, customized fluid replacement programs are recommended… During exercise, consuming beverages containing electrolytes and carbohydrates can provide benefits over water alone under certain circumstances. After exercise, the goal is to replace any fluid electrolyte deficit."

- **Claim:** Sodium is the priority electrolyte to replace because it is the principal electrolyte lost in sweat (in quantities that vary widely between athletes).
  **Source:** Baker LB (2017), "Sweating Rate and Sweat Sodium Concentration in Athletes: A Review of Methodology and Intra/Interindividual Variability." *Sports Med* 47(Suppl 1):111–128. doi:10.1007/s40279-017-0691-5. PMID: 28332116.
  **URL:** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28332116/
  **Verification:** FETCHED & CONFIRMS — Abstract: "Athletes lose water and electrolytes as a consequence of thermoregulatory sweating during exercise… The information gleaned from sweat testing is often used to guide personalized fluid and electrolyte replacement recommendations." The review's focus on sweat **sodium** concentration (the headline electrolyte) supports sodium being the primary electrolyte to replace. (Exact per-serving mg targets are product choices — see GAPS.)

- **Claim:** Carbohydrate aids fuelling during prolonged endurance exercise (and carbohydrate-electrolyte solutions support absorption); intake ~30–60 g/h for longer events.
  **Source:** Burke LM, Hawley JA, Wong SHS, Jeukendrup AE (2011), "Carbohydrates for training and competition." *J Sports Sci* 29 Suppl 1:S17–27. doi:10.1080/02640414.2011.585473. PMID: 21660838.
  **URL:** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21660838/
  **Verification:** FETCHED & CONFIRMS — see verbatim quote under post-workout above ("30–60 g·h⁻¹… events >2.5 h… up to 90 g·h⁻¹. Products containing special blends of different carbohydrates may maximize absorption"). Supports the carbohydrate-for-endurance-fuelling claim.

## protein-bar

- **Claim:** Sugar alcohols (polyols) such as maltitol can cause GI symptoms in a dose-related way when eaten in larger amounts.
  **Source:** Koutsou GA, Storey DM, Lee A, et al. (1996), "Dose-related gastrointestinal response to the ingestion of either isomalt, lactitol or maltitol in milk chocolate." *Eur J Clin Nutr* 50(1):17–21. PMID: 8617186.
  **URL:** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8617186/
  **Verification:** FETCHED & CONFIRMS (with an important honesty caveat) — Randomised double-blind crossover, 59 adults, 40 g polyol in chocolate. Abstract: "Ingestion of 40 g maltitol caused… only flatulence, borborygms and colic (P<0.01), symptoms being rated as only mild. A reduction to 30 g led to a decrease in all symptoms except mild flatulence. Maltitol did not have any laxative effect when ingested at either 30 g or 40 g per day." **Caveat for our copy:** this supports "dose-related, can cause GI upset in larger amounts," but maltitol was the *best-tolerated* polyol tested (milder than lactitol/isomalt, and not laxative at ≤40 g). Avoid overstating maltitol specifically as a strong GI offender — the data show it is comparatively gentle.

- **Claim:** Polyols are incompletely digested, osmotically active carbohydrates — the mechanism behind their laxative/GI effects at higher intakes.
  **Source:** Livesey G (2003), "Health potential of polyols as sugar replacers, with emphasis on low glycaemic properties." *Nutr Res Rev* 16(2):163–91. doi:10.1079/NRR200371. PMID: 19087388.
  **URL:** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19087388/
  **Verification:** FETCHED & CONFIRMS — Abstract describes polyols as "low-digestible (potentially helpful in the colon), osmotic (colon-hydrating, laxative and purifying) carbohydrates." Provides the mechanistic basis for GI/laxative effects at higher doses.

## meal-replacement

- **Claim:** A meal replacement is a portion- and calorie-controlled, nutritionally complete product designed to substitute for a meal, and such products are effective for weight management.
  **Source:** Astbury NM, Piernas C, Hartmann-Boyce J, Lapworth S, Aveyard P, Jebb SA (2019), "A systematic review and meta-analysis of the effectiveness of meal replacements for weight loss." *Obes Rev* 20(4):569–587. doi:10.1111/obr.12816. PMID: 30675990.
  **URL:** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30675990/ (PMC: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6849863/)
  **Verification:** FETCHED & CONFIRMS — Abstract: across 23 RCTs / 7884 adults, "Programmes incorporating meal replacements led to greater weight loss at 1 year than comparator weight loss programmes and should be considered as a valid option for management of overweight and obesity." Establishes MR as a defined, effective food category (distinct from an ad-hoc protein shake). **Note:** the precise compositional thresholds in our copy (protein+carb+fat+micros+fibre, ~300–400 kcal/serving) are a regulatory/definitional standard, not from this meta-analysis — see GAPS.

## gut-digestion

- **Claim:** Probiotic benefits are strain-specific and dose-dependent — a probiotic must be a defined live organism delivered in an adequate amount (hence naming the strain and CFU matters).
  **Source:** Hill C, Guarner F, Reid G, et al. (2014), "Expert consensus document. The International Scientific Association for Probiotics and Prebiotics consensus statement on the scope and appropriate use of the term probiotic." *Nat Rev Gastroenterol Hepatol* 11(8):506–14. doi:10.1038/nrgastro.2014.66. PMID: 24912386.
  **URL:** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24912386/
  **Verification:** FETCHED & CONFIRMS — Abstract reaffirms the FAO/WHO definition: "live microorganisms which when administered in adequate amounts confer a health benefit on the host" and states "A more precise use of the term 'probiotic' will be useful to guide clinicians and consumers in differentiating the diverse products on the market." Supports strain-/dose-specificity ("adequate amounts", defined organisms).

- **Claim:** Prebiotic fibre (e.g. inulin) feeds beneficial gut bacteria — a prebiotic is a substrate selectively used by host microbes to confer a health benefit.
  **Source:** Gibson GR, Hutkins R, Sanders ME, et al. (2017), "Expert consensus document: The International Scientific Association for Probiotics and Prebiotics (ISAPP) consensus statement on the definition and scope of prebiotics." *Nat Rev Gastroenterol Hepatol* 14(8):491–502. doi:10.1038/nrgastro.2017.75. PMID: 28611480.
  **URL:** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28611480/
  **Verification:** FETCHED & CONFIRMS — Abstract: prebiotic defined as "a substrate that is selectively utilized by host microorganisms conferring a health benefit… The requirement for selective microbiota-mediated mechanisms was retained." Inulin-type fructans are the archetypal prebiotic covered by this consensus.

- **Claim:** Lactase (the enzyme) breaks down lactose, so lactase supplements help people with lactose intolerance digest dairy.
  **Source:** National Institute of Diabetes and Digestive and Kidney Diseases (NIDDK / NIH), "Treatment for Lactose Intolerance" (last reviewed Feb 2018).
  **URL:** https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/digestive-diseases/lactose-intolerance/treatment
  **Verification:** FETCHED & CONFIRMS — Page text: "Lactase products are tablets or drops that contain lactase, the enzyme that breaks down lactose. You can take lactase tablets before you eat or drink milk products… The lactase breaks down the lactose in foods and drinks, lowering your chances of having lactose intolerance symptoms." Authoritative US government source.

- **Claim:** (Supporting mechanism) Lactose intolerance results from deficiency of the lactase enzyme; managing lactose digestion addresses the symptoms.
  **Source:** Leis R, de Castro MJ, de Lamas C, Picáns R, Couce ML (2020), "Effects of Prebiotic and Probiotic Supplementation on Lactase Deficiency and Lactose Intolerance: A Systematic Review of Controlled Trials." *Nutrients* 12(5):1487. doi:10.3390/nu12051487. PMID: 32443748.
  **URL:** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32443748/
  **Verification:** FETCHED & CONFIRMS — Abstract: "Lactose intolerance (LI) is characterized by the presence of primarily gastrointestinal clinical signs resulting from colonic fermentation of lactose, the absorption of which is impaired due to a deficiency in the lactase enzyme." Confirms the lactase-deficiency mechanism behind LI.

- **Claim:** Most people under-consume fibre relative to recommendations.
  **Source:** NHS, "How to get more fibre into your diet" (Live Well / Eat Well / Digestive health).
  **URL:** https://www.nhs.uk/live-well/eat-well/digestive-health/how-to-get-more-fibre-into-your-diet/
  **Verification:** FETCHED & CONFIRMS — Page text: "Government guidelines say our dietary fibre intake should increase to 30g a day… As most adults are only eating an average of about 20g a day, we need to find ways of increasing our intake." Directly supports fibre under-consumption.

## UNVERIFIED / GAPS

These claims could **not** be backed by a fetched, authoritative source within this pass. They are flagged rather than padded with weak or off-target citations.

- **casein — "micellar casein is higher quality than calcium caseinate":** No direct, verified head-to-head study found comparing micellar casein vs calcium caseinate on digestion rate or "quality." The Jäger 2017 ISSN stand covers protein quality generally, but I did not verify a specific micellar-vs-caseinate comparison. *Recommendation:* soften to "micellar casein retains its native micelle structure and digests slowly (Boirie 1997)," or commission a targeted search for a micellar-vs-caseinate aminoacidemia study before asserting a quality ranking.

- **casein — "~24–25 g protein per scoop":** This is a product-label/typical-serving figure, not a research finding. No scientific citation is appropriate; cite representative product labels instead.

- **post-workout — exact "carbohydrate ~0.5–1 g/kg refills muscle glycogen":** The *principle* (carbohydrate refuels glycogen) is verified via Burke 2011 (PMID 21660838) and the Kerksick 2017 ISSN nutrient-timing stand (PMID 28919842). The specific per-kg post-exercise dose was not a verbatim figure in the fetched abstracts (Burke gives g/h *during* exercise; rapid-recovery glycogen guidance is ~1.2 g/kg/h in the full texts). *Recommendation:* attribute the per-kg number to the full text of Kerksick 2017 / Burke 2011 after reading the PMC full text, or present it as a practical guideline.

- **hydration — "sodium 500–1000 mg per serving" and "potassium & magnesium support fluid balance":** Sodium being the primary sweat electrolyte is verified (Baker 2017, ACSM 2007), but the exact 500–1000 mg/serving figure is a product-formulation target, not a cited research value. The potassium/magnesium fluid-balance claim is physiologically standard but I could **not** fetch-verify a clean source (NIH ODS Potassium/Magnesium fact sheets returned HTTP 403 / Cloudflare block on this pass). *Recommendation:* retry NIH ODS fact sheets (Potassium-HealthProfessional, Magnesium-HealthProfessional) from a different fetch path before citing specific roles.

- **protein-bar — "protein-to-calorie ratio (~15–20 g protein per ~200 kcal) is the key quality marker":** This is a sensible practical heuristic but is editorial; no single research paper defines a protein-bar quality threshold this way. It can be supported indirectly by protein-requirement literature (Witard 2014; Morton 2018) but there is no direct citation for the ratio rule itself. *Recommendation:* present as The Lifting Lab's own quality rubric, not as a cited scientific standard.

- **meal-replacement — precise composition standard ("protein+carbs+fat+broad micronutrients+fibre, ~300–400 kcal/serving"):** The historical UK/EU regulatory standard exists — *The Foods Intended for Use in Energy Restricted Diets for Weight Reduction Regulations 1997* (SI 1997/2182), implementing EU Directive 96/8/EC — and its title/existence was verified at https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/1997/2182/contents/made. However, I could **not** fetch-verify the exact Schedule text (the ~200–400 kcal per meal, protein 25–50% of energy, and mandatory vitamin/mineral minimums) — the schedule/contents body did not render in the fetch. Astbury 2019 confirms MR are calorie-controlled nutritionally-complete products but does not state the per-serving kcal/composition thresholds. *Recommendation:* verify the SI 1997/2182 Schedule (or the EFSA opinion on meal replacement composition) before quoting specific kcal/macro thresholds.

- **gut-digestion — "digestive enzymes protease/lipase aid digestion of specific foods":** Only the **lactase → lactose** link was verified (NIDDK; Leis 2020). The broader claim that supplemental protease/lipase aid digestion of protein/fat in people without pancreatic insufficiency was not separately verified. *Recommendation:* keep the strong, evidence-backed lactase example; treat general protease/lipase digestive-aid claims cautiously or source a specific trial (e.g. pancreatic enzyme replacement literature) before asserting.
