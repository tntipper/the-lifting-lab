# Guide Citations — Batch A (creatine, whey, whey-isolate, pre-workout, eaas, intra-workout)

> Every citation below was confirmed by fetching its URL and reading enough of the source to verify it supports the stated claim. Verification notes quote or paraphrase the supporting text. Claims that could not be backed by a fetched authoritative source are listed honestly in the **UNVERIFIED / GAPS** section — none were padded with plausible-looking references.

## creatine

- **Claim:** 3–5 g/day is the effective maintenance dose once muscle stores are saturated.
  **Source:** Kreider RB, et al. (2017). "International Society of Sports Nutrition position stand: safety and efficacy of creatine supplementation in exercise, sport, and medicine." *J Int Soc Sports Nutr* 14:18. DOI: 10.1186/s12970-017-0173-z. PMID: 28615996.
  **URL:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5469049/
  **Verification:** FETCHED & CONFIRMS — "Once muscle creatine stores are fully saturated, creatine stores can generally be maintained by ingesting 3–5 g/day, although some studies indicate that larger athletes may need to ingest as much as 5–10 g/day."

- **Claim:** Optional loading of ~20 g/day for ~1 week rapidly saturates muscle stores.
  **Source:** Kreider RB, et al. (2017), ISSN creatine position stand. DOI: 10.1186/s12970-017-0173-z.
  **URL:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5469049/
  **Verification:** FETCHED & CONFIRMS — "The most effective way to increase muscle creatine stores is to ingest 5 g of creatine monohydrate (or approximately 0.3 g/kg body weight) four times daily for 5–7 days" (i.e. ~20 g/day for ~1 week). An alternative slow protocol of "3 g/day of creatine monohydrate for 28 days" is also described.

- **Claim:** Monohydrate has the deepest evidence base.
  **Source:** Kreider RB, et al. (2017), ISSN creatine position stand. DOI: 10.1186/s12970-017-0173-z.
  **URL:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5469049/
  **Verification:** FETCHED & CONFIRMS — "The most commonly studied form of creatine in the literature is creatine monohydrate"; "The vast majority of studies assessing the efficacy of creatine supplementation on muscle phosphagen levels, whole body creatine retention, and/or performance have evaluated creatine monohydrate."

- **Claim:** HCl / ethyl-ester / buffered / other forms show no proven superiority over monohydrate.
  **Source:** Kreider RB, et al. (2017), ISSN creatine position stand. DOI: 10.1186/s12970-017-0173-z.
  **URL:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5469049/
  **Verification:** FETCHED & CONFIRMS — "Claims that different forms of creatine are degraded to a lesser degree than creatine monohydrate in vivo or result in a greater uptake to muscle are currently unfounded. Clinical evidence has not demonstrated that different forms of creatine such as creatine citrate, creatine serum, creatine ethyl ester, buffered forms of creatine, or creatine nitrate promote greater creatine retention than creatine monohydrate." (Note: this list does not explicitly name "HCl"; the stand's general conclusion covers alternative forms, but HCl is not individually cited — see GAPS.)

## whey

- **Claim:** Total protein target ~0.8–1 g per lb bodyweight/day for building/maintaining muscle.
  **Source:** Jäger R, et al. (2017). "International Society of Sports Nutrition Position Stand: protein and exercise." *J Int Soc Sports Nutr* 14:20. DOI: 10.1186/s12970-017-0177-8. PMID: 28642676.
  **URL:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5477153/
  **Verification:** FETCHED & CONFIRMS — "an overall daily protein intake in the range of 1.4–2.0 g protein/kg body weight/day (g/kg/d) is sufficient for most exercising individuals." (1.4–2.0 g/kg ≈ 0.64–0.91 g/lb; the guide's "~0.8–1 g/lb" sits at/just above the upper end — accurate as a practical muscle-building target.)

- **Claim:** Whey is an effective, practical protein source; supplementation reliably supports intake.
  **Source:** Jäger R, et al. (2017), ISSN protein position stand. DOI: 10.1186/s12970-017-0177-8.
  **URL:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5477153/
  **Verification:** FETCHED & CONFIRMS — "supplementation is a practical way of ensuring intake of adequate protein quality and quantity, while minimizing caloric intake"; "Rapidly digested proteins that contain high proportions of essential amino acids (EAAs) and adequate leucine, are most effective in stimulating MPS." (Whey is the canonical rapidly-digested high-EAA/high-leucine protein. NOTE: the specific "concentrate = ~70–80% protein / good value" figure is standard dairy-industry composition but is NOT a numeric quote from this stand — see GAPS.)

- **Claim:** Informed Sport batch testing matters for drug-tested athletes.
  **Source:** Informed Sport (LGC Group) — official certification program site.
  **URL:** https://sport.wetestyoutrust.com/ (also reached via informed-sport.com)
  **Verification:** FETCHED & CONFIRMS — "Informed Sport is the world's leading testing and certification program for brands producing sports and nutritional supplements. Designed for elite sport, it protects athletes from inadvertent doping caused by supplements contaminated with banned substances." Page cites "As many as one-in-ten supplements are contaminated" and that "product contamination was responsible for 8% of all anti-doping violations between 2005-2022" (Anti-Doping Knowledge Centre). Certification detail at https://sport.wetestyoutrust.com/about/certification-process.

## whey-isolate

- **Claim:** Isolate is more filtered — higher protein concentration with lower lactose (and fat).
  **Source (lactose):** Huppertz T, Gazi I. (2016). "Lactose in dairy ingredients: Effect on processing and storage stability." *J Dairy Sci* 99(8):6842–6851. DOI: 10.3168/jds.2015-10033. PMID: 26387022.
  **URL:** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26387022/
  **Verification:** FETCHED & CONFIRMS — lactose "is present in virtually all dry dairy ingredients, with levels ranging from <2% (e.g., caseinates, milk protein isolates) to 100% in lactose powders." Confirms isolate-grade dairy proteins carry minimal lactose. (Supports the "lower lactose" half directly; the "90%+ protein / lower fat" composition figures are standard spec but not numerically quoted here — see GAPS.)

- **Claim:** Hydrolysed whey's faster absorption rarely changes outcomes for typical lifters.
  **Source:** Jäger R, et al. (2017), ISSN protein position stand. DOI: 10.1186/s12970-017-0177-8.
  **URL:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5477153/
  **Verification:** FETCHED — PARTIAL. The stand confirms that rapidly-digested, high-EAA, high-leucine proteins (whey, including hydrolysate) most effectively stimulate MPS, and that "Different types and quality of protein can affect amino acid bioavailability." However, a direct head-to-head source showing hydrolysate vs. intact whey produces *equivalent long-term hypertrophy/strength outcomes in trained lifters* was NOT located this session (targeted PubMed query returned zero results). Treat the "rarely changes outcomes" framing as not yet citation-backed — see GAPS.

## pre-workout

- **Claim:** Caffeine ~3–6 mg/kg bodyweight improves performance / perceived effort.
  **Source:** Guest NS, et al. (2021). "International society of sports nutrition position stand: caffeine and exercise performance." *J Int Soc Sports Nutr* 18:1. DOI: 10.1186/s12970-020-00383-4. PMID: 33388079.
  **URL:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7777221/
  **Verification:** FETCHED & CONFIRMS — "Caffeine has consistently been shown to improve exercise performance when consumed in doses of 3–6 mg/kg body mass. Minimal effective doses... may be as low as 2 mg/kg." Benefits span "muscular endurance, movement velocity and muscular strength, sprinting, jumping..." Most common timing "60 min pre-exercise."

- **Claim:** Very high caffeine doses raise side-effect risk without added benefit.
  **Source:** Guest NS, et al. (2021), ISSN caffeine position stand. DOI: 10.1186/s12970-020-00383-4.
  **URL:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7777221/
  **Verification:** FETCHED & CONFIRMS — "Very high doses of caffeine (e.g. 9 mg/kg) are associated with a high incidence of side-effects and do not seem to be required to elicit an ergogenic effect." (NOTE: ISSN frames the high-dose threshold as ~9 mg/kg, NOT the absolute ">400 mg" figure used in the guide. The 400 mg figure aligns with EFSA/FDA single-dose safety guidance rather than this stand — adjust guide wording or add an EFSA cite.)

- **Claim:** Citrulline (malate 6–8 g) supports performance / reduces fatigue & perceived effort.
  **Source 1:** Vårvik FT, Bjørnsen T, Gonzalez AM. (2021). "Acute Effect of Citrulline Malate on Repetition Performance During Strength Training: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis." *Int J Sport Nutr Exerc Metab* 31(4):350–358. DOI: 10.1123/ijsnem.2020-0295. PMID: 34010809.
  **URL:** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34010809/
  **Verification:** FETCHED & CONFIRMS — "Supplementing with 6–8 g of CitMal 40–60 min before exercise increased repetitions by 3 ± 5 (6.4 ± 7.9%) compared with placebo (p = .022)... Acute CitMal supplementation may, therefore, delay fatigue and enhance muscle endurance during high-intensity strength training." (Small but significant ergogenic effect — matches the guide's measured framing.)
  **Source 2:** Rhim HC, et al. (2020). "Effect of citrulline on post-exercise rating of perceived exertion, muscle soreness, and blood lactate levels: A systematic review and meta-analysis." *J Sport Health Sci* 9(6):553–561. DOI: 10.1016/j.jshs.2020.02.003. PMID: 33308806; PMCID: PMC7749242.
  **URL:** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33308806/
  **Verification:** FETCHED & CONFIRMS — "Citrulline supplements significantly reduced post-exercise RPE and muscle soreness without affecting blood lactate levels." Most frequent dose was 8 g citrulline malate. Supports the "perceived effort / recovery" angle. (NOTE: the pure L-citrulline ~3–4 g dose claim is plausible but these meta-analyses are dominated by citrulline-*malate* at 6–8 g; an L-citrulline-specific dose source was not separately verified — see GAPS.)

- **Claim:** Beta-alanine buffers fatigue in higher-rep/sustained work and causes paraesthesia (tingling).
  **Source:** Trexler ET, et al. (2015). "International society of sports nutrition position stand: Beta-Alanine." *J Int Soc Sports Nutr* 12:30. DOI: 10.1186/s12970-015-0090-y. PMID: 26175657.
  **URL:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4501114/
  **Verification:** FETCHED & CONFIRMS — "Four weeks of beta-alanine supplementation (4–6 g daily) significantly augments muscle carnosine concentrations, thereby acting as an intracellular pH buffer"; performance benefits are "more pronounced... in open end-point tasks/time trials lasting 1 to 4 min in duration"; "The only reported side effect is paraesthesia (tingling), but studies indicate this can be attenuated by using divided lower doses (1.6 g) or using a sustained-release formula." (NOTE: ISSN recommends **4–6 g/day**, whereas the guide states **3.2 g/day**. 3.2 g/day is a common per-study minimum but is below the ISSN-recommended range — recommend updating the guide to 4–6 g/day, or note 3.2 g as a lower maintenance dose.)

## eaas

- **Claim:** Muscle protein synthesis requires all 9 essential amino acids.
  **Source:** Wolfe RR. (2017). "Branched-chain amino acids and muscle protein synthesis in humans: myth or reality?" *J Int Soc Sports Nutr* 14:30. DOI: 10.1186/s12970-017-0184-9. PMID: 28852372; PMCID: PMC5568273.
  **URL:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5568273/
  **Verification:** FETCHED & CONFIRMS — "Nine of the twenty are considered essential amino acids (EAAs)... For synthesis of new muscle protein, all the EAAs, along with the eleven non-essential amino acids... must be present in adequate amounts"; "Muscle protein synthesis will be limited by the lack of availability of any of the EAAs." Reinforced by Jäger 2017 stand: "it is the EAAs that are required to stimulate MPS."

- **Claim:** Leucine ~2–3 g (≈0.7–3 g) is the practical trigger range for MPS.
  **Source:** Jäger R, et al. (2017), ISSN protein position stand. DOI: 10.1186/s12970-017-0177-8.
  **URL:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5477153/
  **Verification:** FETCHED & CONFIRMS — "Acute protein doses should strive to contain 700–3000 mg of leucine and/or a higher relative leucine content, in addition to a balanced array of the essential amino acids (EAAs)." (700–3000 mg = 0.7–3 g; the guide's "~2–3 g leucine trigger" is at the upper, more reliably-effective end of this range. Wolfe 2017 adds that leucine acts as "a regulator of intracellular signaling pathways... involved in protein synthesis.")

- **Claim:** BCAAs alone are insufficient to maximise MPS.
  **Source:** Wolfe RR. (2017), JISSN. DOI: 10.1186/s12970-017-0184-9.
  **URL:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5568273/
  **Verification:** FETCHED & CONFIRMS — "we conclude that the claim that consumption of dietary BCAAs stimulates muscle protein synthesis or produces an anabolic response in human subjects is unwarranted." In the post-absorptive state, BCAAs alone cannot exceed the supply of the other EAAs (only available from muscle breakdown), so MPS cannot be maximised.

## intra-workout

- **Claim:** EAAs are preferable to BCAAs intra-workout (same MPS rationale).
  **Source:** Wolfe RR. (2017), JISSN. DOI: 10.1186/s12970-017-0184-9.
  **URL:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5568273/
  **Verification:** FETCHED & CONFIRMS — because all nine EAAs must be present to synthesise new muscle protein and BCAAs alone do not produce an anabolic response, a full-spectrum EAA blend is the rationally superior choice over BCAAs for stimulating MPS around training.

- **Claim:** Sodium is the key electrolyte to replace during/around training.
  **Source:** American College of Sports Medicine; Sawka MN, et al. (2007). "American College of Sports Medicine position stand. Exercise and fluid replacement." *Med Sci Sports Exerc* 39(2):377–390. DOI: 10.1249/mss.0b013e31802ca597. PMID: 17277604.
  **URL:** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17277604/
  **Verification:** FETCHED & CONFIRMS — goal is to start exercise "euhydrated and with normal plasma electrolyte levels" and to prevent "excessive changes in electrolyte balance"; "During exercise, consuming beverages containing electrolytes and carbohydrates can provide benefits over water alone." The stand emphasises sweat sodium losses and customised replacement. (Sodium is the principal electrolyte lost in sweat; this stand is the authoritative basis. NOTE: it discusses "electrolytes" broadly with sodium as primary, rather than isolating sodium by name in the abstract.)

## UNVERIFIED / GAPS

Claims I could NOT back with a fetched authoritative source this session (no fabrication — flagged honestly):

1. **creatine — timing has little effect on outcomes.** Not explicitly stated in the section of the Kreider 2017 stand I read. The stand covers dose, loading, and forms thoroughly but I did not capture an explicit "timing is largely irrelevant" statement. NEEDS a dedicated source (e.g., Antonio & Ciccone 2013, pre- vs post-workout creatine) — verify before citing.
2. **creatine — "HCl" specifically.** The ISSN stand's no-superiority conclusion names citrate, serum, ethyl ester, buffered, and nitrate forms — but not creatine HCl by name. The general conclusion reasonably extends to HCl, but no source individually tested HCl was verified.
3. **whey — concentrate = ~70–80% protein / good value.** This is standard dairy-spec composition, but I did not verify a source stating the 70–80% figure. The Jäger stand supports whey's effectiveness/value in general terms only. (A USDA FoodData or whey-processing review would close this.)
4. **whey — amino / nitrogen "spiking" misrepresents protein content.** PubMed returned ZERO results for protein/nitrogen-spiking queries. This is primarily a regulatory/industry-journalism topic (FDA, trade press, class-action coverage), not peer-reviewed literature. No authoritative academic source verified — do not cite as peer-reviewed.
5. **whey-isolate — 90%+ protein / lower fat numeric spec.** Lactose claim is verified (Huppertz 2016: isolates <2% lactose); the explicit "≥90% protein, lower fat" numbers are standard spec but not numerically quoted in a fetched source.
6. **whey-isolate — hydrolysate faster absorption "rarely changes outcomes."** Targeted PubMed query returned zero results. Jäger 2017 supports that rapid digestion aids MPS but a head-to-head long-term outcome-equivalence study in trained lifters was not located. PARTIAL only.
7. **pre-workout — pure L-citrulline ~3–4 g dose.** Verified citrulline evidence is dominated by citrulline-*malate* at 6–8 g (Vårvik 2021, Rhim 2020). An L-citrulline-specific 3–4 g dosing source was not separately verified.
8. **pre-workout — caffeine ">400 mg" high-dose threshold.** ISSN frames the high-dose risk as ~9 mg/kg, not an absolute 400 mg. The 400 mg figure matches EFSA/FDA single-dose guidance — cite EFSA "Scientific Opinion on the safety of caffeine" if the 400 mg number is kept.
9. **pre-workout — beta-alanine 3.2 g/day.** ISSN recommends **4–6 g/day**; 3.2 g/day is below the recommended range. Recommend updating the guide figure (or labelling 3.2 g as a lower/maintenance dose).
10. **intra-workout — cyclic dextrin (HBCD) / maltodextrin as fast intra carbs.** No strong systematic source fetched/verified this session. Highly-branched cyclic dextrin has limited and mixed primary evidence; do not present as well-established without a verified source.
