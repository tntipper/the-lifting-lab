# Guide Citations — Batch C (hormone-support, cycle-support, vitamin, multivitamin, vitamin-d, zma)

> **COMPLIANCE NOTE:** This is the most sensitive group (hormones, organ health). Framing is **educational, not medical advice**. Where evidence is weak, mixed, or merely mechanistic, this is stated explicitly rather than overstated. Low testosterone, liver/organ concerns, and anabolic-steroid use should be assessed by a clinician with bloodwork — supplements are not a substitute.
>

## hormone-support

- **Claim:** OTC "testosterone boosters" do not reliably raise testosterone in healthy men.
  **Source:** Balasubramanian A, Thirumavalavan N, Srivatsav A, Yu J, Lipshultz LI, Pastuszak AW. (2019). "Testosterone Imposters: An Analysis of Popular Online Testosterone Boosting Supplements." *J Sex Med* 16(2):203–212. DOI: 10.1016/j.jsxm.2018.12.008. PMID: 30770069. PMCID: PMC6407704.
  **URL:** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30770069/
  **Verification:** FETCHED & CONFIRMS — across the 10 most common T-booster ingredients, literature review found "191 studies… Among 37 human studies, 30% observed an increase in T levels, 3% a decrease, 46% no effect, and 22% were indeterminate." So the majority of human data showed no increase, and consumer benefit reports collapsed after filtering fake reviews (e.g. "91% decrease in users reporting increased libido" once disingenuous reviews were removed). Supports the claim that OTC T-boosters do not reliably raise testosterone.

- **Claim:** Ashwagandha has evidence for reducing stress and may have modest effects on testosterone in some trials.
  **Source:** National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health (NCCIH), NIH. "Ashwagandha." (evidence summary; reviewed current as of fetch date).
  **URL:** https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/ashwagandha
  **Verification:** FETCHED & CONFIRMS — "Research shows that some ashwagandha preparations may be effective for insomnia and stress. However, evidence is unclear about its effects on anxiety. There is some limited evidence that suggests that taking ashwagandha for 2 to 4 months may increase testosterone levels and sperm quality." Note the testosterone signal is explicitly framed by NIH as *limited* — matches a conservative "modest in some trials" framing, not a strong claim.

- **Claim:** Tribulus terrestris has largely failed to show a testosterone effect in controlled studies.
  **Source:** Ho CY, Hsu CH, Chien TJ. (2026). "Herbal dietary supplements for erectile dysfunction: A systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized-controlled trials." *J Tradit Complement Med* 16(1):109–120. DOI: 10.1016/j.jtcme.2025.11.001. PMID: 41696741. PMCID: PMC12902307.
  **URL:** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41696741/
  **Verification:** FETCHED & CONFIRMS — meta-analysis of 14 RCTs (1,227 men). While ginseng raised serum testosterone, "evidence for other agents such as *Tribulus terrestris* and Maca remains insufficient, underscoring the need for larger, high-quality trials." Supports that tribulus has not demonstrated a reliable testosterone effect.

## cycle-support

> **Framing:** harm-reduction / educational. The core honest message — that supplements do **not** neutralise the organ risk of anabolic-steroid use, and that bloodwork + medical supervision are what protect health — is partly an editorial synthesis; the organ-harm half is well documented (see GAPS for the AAS-specific source blocked by the outage). Liver "support" supplements have weaker evidence than marketing implies.

- **Claim:** Milk thistle (silymarin) is studied for liver support, but high-quality evidence of benefit is lacking.
  **Source:** National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health (NCCIH), NIH. "Milk Thistle." (evidence summary).
  **URL:** https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/milk-thistle
  **Verification:** FETCHED & CONFIRMS — "There isn't enough high-quality evidence to allow definite conclusions… Results from clinical trials of milk thistle for liver diseases, such as alcohol-related liver disease, hepatitis B and C, non-alcoholic fatty liver disease… have been conflicting or too limited to allow conclusions to be reached. Two studies funded by [NCCIH] — one on hepatitis C and one on non-alcoholic steatohepatitis — did not show benefits from supplementation with the milk thistle extract silymarin." Honestly supports "studied for liver support" while making clear benefit is unproven.

- **Claim:** Omega-3 (EPA/DHA) must be obtained from diet/supplements because the body converts little from plant ALA — relevant to those eating little oily fish.
  **Source:** NIH Office of Dietary Supplements. "Omega-3 Fatty Acids — Fact Sheet for Health Professionals."
  **URL:** https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Omega3FattyAcids-HealthProfessional/ (verified via Wayback snapshot: http://web.archive.org/web/2024/https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Omega3FattyAcids-HealthProfessional/)
  **Verification:** FETCHED & CONFIRMS (Wayback snapshot of the NIH ODS page) — "ALA can be converted into EPA and then to DHA, but the conversion (which occurs primarily in the liver) is very limited, with reported rates of less than 15%. Therefore, consuming EPA and DHA directly from foods and/or dietary supplements is the only practical way to increase levels of these fatty acids in the body." Supports the rationale for fish-oil/omega-3 supplementation in low-oily-fish diets. (NOTE: this fetch confirms the dietary-need rationale; the *cardiovascular-marker* benefit half of the guide claim was not verified this session — see GAPS.)

## vitamin

- **Claim:** Vitamin D supplementation is widely recommended for UK adults because of limited winter sunlight.
  **Source:** NHS. "Vitamins and minerals — Vitamin D." (Crown copyright; UK Department of Health and Social Care guidance.)
  **URL:** https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/vitamins-and-minerals/vitamin-d/
  **Verification:** FETCHED & CONFIRMS — "between October and early March we do not make enough vitamin D from sunlight"; "everyone (including pregnant and breastfeeding women) should consider taking a daily supplement containing 10 micrograms of vitamin D during the autumn and winter." Adults need 10 µg (= 400 IU) per day.

- **Claim:** Omega-3 is worth supplementing for people who eat little oily fish.
  **Source:** NIH ODS Omega-3 fact sheet (as above) + NHS vitamin D page food-source list.
  **URL:** https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Omega3FattyAcids-HealthProfessional/ (Wayback-verified)
  **Verification:** FETCHED & CONFIRMS — oily fish (salmon, sardines, mackerel, etc.) is the principal dietary EPA/DHA source (NHS lists "oily fish – such as salmon, sardines, trout, herring or mackerel"); ODS confirms direct EPA/DHA intake is "the only practical way to increase levels." Supports supplementation for low-oily-fish diets.

- **Claim:** Fat-soluble vitamin A can be harmful in excess.
  **Source:** NHS. "Vitamins and minerals — Vitamin A."
  **URL:** https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/vitamins-and-minerals/vitamin-a/
  **Verification:** FETCHED & CONFIRMS — "having more than an average of 1.5 mg (1,500 µg) a day of vitamin A over many years may affect your bones, making them more likely to fracture when you're older… make sure your daily intake from food and supplements does not exceed 1.5 mg (1,500 µg)."

- **Claim:** Fat-soluble vitamin E can be harmful in excess.
  **Source:** NHS. "Vitamins and minerals — Vitamin E."
  **URL:** https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/vitamins-and-minerals/vitamin-e/
  **Verification:** FETCHED & CONFIRMS — "If you take vitamin E supplements, do not take too much as this could be harmful. Taking 540mg (800 IU) or less a day of vitamin E supplements is unlikely to cause any harm." (NHS notes high-dose evidence is limited but advises a ceiling.)

- **Claim:** Iron can be harmful in excess.
  **Source:** NHS. "Vitamins and minerals — Iron."
  **URL:** https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/vitamins-and-minerals/iron/
  **Verification:** FETCHED & CONFIRMS — "Side effects of taking high doses (over 20mg) of iron include constipation, feeling sick, being sick, stomach pain… Very high doses of iron can be fatal, particularly if taken by children." Most people get enough from diet; supplements only if advised.

## multivitamin

- **Claim:** Avoid megadoses of fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K), which accumulate and can be harmful.
  **Source:** NHS vitamins & minerals pages — Vitamin A, Vitamin D, Vitamin E.
  **URL:** https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/vitamins-and-minerals/vitamin-a/ ; https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/vitamins-and-minerals/vitamin-d/ ; https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/vitamins-and-minerals/vitamin-e/
  **Verification:** FETCHED & CONFIRMS — Vitamin A: excess over years "may affect your bones"; cap intake at 1.5 mg/day. Vitamin D: "Do not take more than 100 micrograms (4,000 IU) of vitamin D a day as it could be harmful"; long-term excess causes hypercalcaemia that "can weaken the bones and damage the kidneys and the heart." Vitamin E: "do not take too much as this could be harmful." All three are fat-soluble and stored, supporting the "no megadoses" guidance. (Vitamin K not separately fetched — see GAPS.)

- **Claim:** Iron should not be included/supplemented without a tested need.
  **Source:** NHS. "Vitamins and minerals — Iron."
  **URL:** https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/vitamins-and-minerals/iron/
  **Verification:** FETCHED & CONFIRMS — "You should be able to get all the iron you need from your daily diet"; supplements are advised mainly for those at higher risk (e.g. heavy periods) and "Speak to a GP or a registered dietitian for more advice." Excess iron is harmful/can be fatal. Supports not adding iron blindly.

## vitamin-d

- **Claim:** UK guidance is ~400 IU (10 micrograms)/day as the minimum.
  **Source:** NHS. "Vitamins and minerals — Vitamin D."
  **URL:** https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/vitamins-and-minerals/vitamin-d/
  **Verification:** FETCHED & CONFIRMS — "Children from the age of 1 year and adults need 10 micrograms (mcg) of vitamin D a day… 10 micrograms of vitamin D is equal to 400 IU."

- **Claim:** 4000 IU/day is the recognised safe upper limit for unsupervised use.
  **Source:** NHS. "Vitamins and minerals — Vitamin D."
  **URL:** https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/vitamins-and-minerals/vitamin-d/
  **Verification:** FETCHED & CONFIRMS — "Do not take more than 100 micrograms (4,000 IU) of vitamin D a day as it could be harmful. This applies to adults, including pregnant and breastfeeding women and the elderly, and children aged 11 to 17 years." Excess over time causes hypercalcaemia.

- **Claim:** D3 (cholecalciferol) raises blood 25(OH)D more effectively than D2.
  **Source:** van den Heuvel EGHM, Lips P, Schoonmade LJ, Lanham-New SA, van Schoor NM. (2024). "Comparison of the Effect of Daily Vitamin D2 and Vitamin D3 Supplementation on Serum 25-Hydroxyvitamin D Concentration… : A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis." *Adv Nutr* 15(1):100133. DOI: 10.1016/j.advnut.2023.09.016. PMID: 37865222. PMCID: PMC10831883.
  **URL:** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37865222/
  **Verification:** FETCHED & CONFIRMS — "Overall, the results based on 20 comparative studies showed vitamin D3 to be superior to vitamin D2 in raising total 25(OH)D concentrations… Vitamin D3 leads to a greater increase of 25(OH)D than vitamin D2, even if limited to daily dose studies." (WMD ~10.4 nmol/L lower for D2.)
  **Secondary support:** Alnafisah RY, et al. (2024). "The Impact and Efficacy of Vitamin D Fortification." *Nutrients* 16(24):4322. DOI: 10.3390/nu16244322. PMID: 39770943 — systematic review noting "vitamin D3 showing superior efficacy over vitamin D2." URL: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39770943/ (FETCHED & CONFIRMS.)

- **Claim:** Common effective supplemental range is ~1000–4000 IU/day.
  **Source:** NHS vitamin D page (bounds).
  **URL:** https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/vitamins-and-minerals/vitamin-d/
  **Verification:** FETCHED — PARTIAL / EDITORIAL. NHS confirms the **floor** (10 µg/400 IU is "enough for most people") and the **ceiling** (do not exceed 4,000 IU unsupervised). The specific "1000–4000 IU is the common effective range" is a practitioner-style framing that sits *within* these verified NHS bounds but is not a verbatim NHS recommendation — present as a practical range, not as official UK guidance.

## zma

- **Claim:** Excess copper/zinc imbalance — high copper intake is harmful (copper toxicity context).
  **Source:** NHS. "Vitamins and minerals — Others" (copper section).
  **URL:** https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/vitamins-and-minerals/others/
  **Verification:** FETCHED & CONFIRMS (copper toxicity) — "Taking high doses of copper could cause stomach pain, sickness, diarrhoea, [and] damage to the liver and kidneys (if taken for a long time)." NOTE: this confirms copper has a toxicity ceiling, but the *specific* mechanism the guide cites — that **excess zinc impairs copper absorption** — was NOT confirmed from this page in the portion fetched (the zinc subsection sits lower on the page than the fetch reached, and the live ODS Zinc fact sheet was Cloudflare-blocked). The zinc→copper antagonism is listed in GAPS pending verification.

**cycle-support**
- *NAC (N-acetylcysteine) for liver support.* Not verified. NAC's solid evidence base is as the antidote for paracetamol/acetaminophen-induced liver injury; routine "liver support" use in healthy people is weakly evidenced. Needs a fetched source before use (search returned mostly off-target results: respiratory, oncology, mushroom poisoning).
- *Omega-3 and CoQ10 for cardiovascular markers.* Omega-3 dietary rationale verified (above); the cardiovascular-outcome/marker claim was not. Note hard-outcome CVD evidence for omega-3 is genuinely **mixed** (e.g. VITAL/ASCEND null vs REDUCE-IT positive); triglyceride-lowering is the better-supported effect. CoQ10: candidate = Mortensen SA et al. (Q-SYMBIO) 2014, *JACC Heart Fail* 2(6):641–649, PMID 25282031 — not fetched (outage).
- *Supplements do not offset the organ risk of anabolic-steroid use; bloodwork + medical supervision protect health.* Organ-harm half is well documented but the specific source was not fetched. Candidate: Pope HG Jr et al. (2014), "Adverse Health Consequences of Performance-Enhancing Drugs: an Endocrine Society Scientific Statement," *Endocr Rev* 35(3):341–375, PMID 24423981 — not fetched (outage). The "supplements don't offset" clause is an editorial synthesis and should be framed as such.

**vitamin / multivitamin**
- *A nutrient mainly helps when correcting a deficiency* — general principle; needs a specific fetched source (e.g. ODS multivitamin/mineral fact sheet via Wayback; Heffernan 2019).
- *Well-absorbed forms matter (e.g. magnesium glycinate vs oxide).* Not verified — magnesium bioavailability comparison study not fetched this session. (Magnesium oxide's lower bioavailability vs organic salts is well documented in the literature; locate and fetch e.g. a Mg bioavailability RCT/review before citing.)
- *Vitamin K megadose caution* — Vitamin K NHS page not separately fetched (A/D/E were).

**vitamin-d**
- *K2 (MK-7) pairing "directs calcium to bone."* Not verified — this is a **mechanistic/emerging** rationale, weaker than the rest of the vitamin-D evidence; flag as such and verify against a specific review (e.g. on MK-7, matrix Gla protein, and vascular/bone calcium) before presenting as established.
- *Fat-soluble — take with food (improves absorption).* Not verified this session. Candidate: a vitamin-D-with-meal absorption study (e.g. Dawson-Hughes/ Mulligan & Licata) — fetch before citing. (The fat-soluble premise itself is uncontroversial; the "take with largest meal improves levels" specific is the part needing a source.)

**zma**
- *ZMA does not raise testosterone in non-deficient individuals.* Not verified (outage). Candidate: Wilborn CD et al. (2004), "Effects of Zinc Magnesium Aspartate (ZMA) Supplementation on Training Adaptations and Markers of Anabolism and Catabolism," *J Int Soc Sports Nutr* 1(2):12–20, PMID 18500945 — the classic null RCT; abstract fetch failed during outage.
- *Zinc and magnesium support testosterone/sleep mainly when deficient.* Not verified — same candidates as hormone-support (Heffernan 2019; ODS fact sheets).
- *Magnesium is associated with sleep quality.* Not verified — magnesium-and-sleep meta-analysis/review not fetched.
- *Excess zinc interferes with copper absorption.* Partially adjacent (copper toxicity confirmed via NHS) but the specific zinc→copper antagonism not confirmed. Candidate: NIH ODS Zinc HP fact sheet (via Wayback) — "Health Risks from Excessive Zinc" section states high zinc intakes can induce copper deficiency; fetch to confirm before citing.
